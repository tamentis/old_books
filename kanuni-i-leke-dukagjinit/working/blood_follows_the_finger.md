# "Blood follows the finger": did the Ottomans widen the blood feud?

Research notes, 2026-10-16. They accompany the English translation of Gjeçovi's *Kanuni i Lekë Dukagjinit* (1933).

## The rule

- **Albanian (1933 spelling):** „Gjaku shkon per gisht"
- **Modern spelling:** *Gjaku shkon për gisht*
- **Literally:** "the blood goes by the finger". *Gjak*: blood; *shkon*: goes; *për*: by, along, after; *gisht*: finger.

"Blood" is the blood-debt, the victim's family's right to take a life in return. "The finger" is the trigger finger, meaning the killer himself.

The Kanun states the rule in Book Ten:

- **§898:** "In the old kanun of the Mountains of Albania only the killer fell into blood, that is, the one who drew, cocked and fired the gun or any weapon against the man."
- **§899:** the victim's house could not pursue "anyone else among the brothers, nephews or cousins of the man who owed the blood, except the finger – the killer" (*pos gishtit – dorërasit*; *dorërasi* means "the one whose hand struck").
- **§900:** "The later kanun includes the males of the killer's house, down to the infant in the cradle, and the close cousins and nephews, even if they are separated, but for 24 hours, in the heat of the blood." After those 24 hours, sureties are given.

The same rule explains why women are exempt (§57): "blood follows the finger", but "a woman does not fall into blood". If a woman kills, her parents' family answers for it.

## The claim under examination

In his note in Book Twelve, Gjeçovi says that several harsh practices came late. Burning the killer's house is one, which he blames on the Turkish government and on the arrogance of powerful houses. Pursuing all the males of the killer's house is another, along with the "proclamation" of blood against the whole brotherhood. He cites "Blood follows the finger" as proof that the original custom was narrower.

**Question:** is there historical evidence that this widening happened under Ottoman rule?

## Findings

### 1. The evidence for an Ottoman origin is internal and undated

- §898–900 record two layers of oral tradition, an "old" and a "later" kanun, as remembered around 1900. Nothing in them dates the change.
- Gjeçovi cites no source for blaming the Turks. He also had a motive: he wanted to present the Kanun as an ancient, noble, Christian law.

### 2. Kin liability existed outside Ottoman control (confirmed)

Montenegro was self-governing and Orthodox, and never under day-to-day Ottoman administration. Article 39 of Prince Danilo's Code (1855) reads, in translation:

> "Since Montenegrins and Brđani [the highland clans] have the custom of taking revenge not only on the guilty party and the killer but also on his innocent brother, such revenge is from this day strictly forbidden, and whoever kills an innocent man shall be sentenced to death."

Source: quoted in Stanimirović & Divac (2025), citing Jovan R. Bojović, *Zakonik knjaza Danila* (Titograd, 1982), p. 65.

The neighbouring highland clans, outside Ottoman rule, treated revenge on the killer's relatives as established custom. Earlier Montenegrin laws also tried to curb feuds: the *Stega* (1796) and the code of Petar I (1798, revised 1803).

### 3. The Ottoman role was mostly neglect, sometimes manipulation, and late suppression

Stanimirović & Divac (2025) report the following:

- **Neglect:** Ottoman authorities "did not interfere much" in the internal affairs of the highland peoples.
- **Manipulation:** at times they fed feuds for their own ends. For example, they sheltered Montenegrin killers on condition that the fugitive kill another Montenegrin.
- **Indirect rule:** in northern Albania they relied on the highlanders for military support and in return left their self-government and customs alone.
- **Late suppression:**
  - in the mid-19th century the governor of the Shkodër vilayet submitted a draft law against blood feud to the provincial council;
  - a sharia ruling proclaimed a general reconciliation of blood across the districts, villages and highlands of the Shkodër sanjak. The authors' source for this is Hasan Kaleshi (1974).

### 4. Unverified points

- **Ottoman house-burning.** No source found confirms Gjeçovi's claim that the Turkish government introduced the burning of the killer's house. Ottoman punitive expeditions in the highlands are documented, against the Kelmendi in 1638 and in the 1845 revolt, but not as a response to blood feuds.
- **19th-century observers.** I haven't checked whether J. G. von Hahn (1854), Hyacinthe Hecquard (1858) or Edith Durham (*High Albania*, 1909) describe the killer's kin as targets.
- **The Council of Arbën (1703).** It is confirmed as a national Catholic council held under Clement XI, with resolutions in Albanian. I haven't confirmed whether it condemned the blood feud.
- **Medieval highland custom.** No pre-Ottoman text of it survives, so a direct before-and-after comparison is impossible.

## Conclusion

- **The two layers are real.** The Kanun itself records an older rule (only the killer) and a later practice (the males of the house).
- **The Ottoman date is unproven.** It rests on Gjeçovi's unsourced assertion.
- **The only firm legal evidence points the other way.** Danilo's Article 39 shows kin liability as normal custom in a neighbouring highland society the Ottomans did not govern. Kin liability is also the usual form of feud in lineage-based societies generally.
- **The most likely reading:** "Blood follows the finger" was an old ideal, used by Gjeçovi and later lawmakers to argue against the practice of their own day. It does not record a time when that practice was absent.
- **Where the Ottomans fit:** their indirect rule and occasional manipulation probably helped the feud persist. There is no evidence that they created collective liability.
- **House-burning is the exception.** Gjeçovi's narrower claim that it came from the Turkish government is possible, but unverified.
